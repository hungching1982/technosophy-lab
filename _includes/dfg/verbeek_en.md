<i>Design for Green</i> arises from one of the most pressing challenges humanity faces today: how to guide our technological societies in a sustainable direction. In a highly engaged, innovative, and compelling way, Dr. Ching Hung develops an important approach to this challenge. This book investigates how we can harness technology to nudge human behavior toward ecological responsibility without sacrificing autonomy, dignity, and democratic values.

At a time when climate change, biodiversity loss, and resource depletion confront us with the consequences of collective inaction, this book charts an alternative course. Our everyday environments always and inevitably already shape what we do. Design always steers behavior, whether we acknowledge it or not. The crucial question, then, is not whether it is desirable at all to influence people’s behavior, but rather how, by whom, and to what ends. Ching Hung invites us to confront this question openly and to approach “design for green” as an ethical and political project as much as a technological one.

In this book, Ching Hung distinguishes two types of behavior-steering technologies: “informational” and “material.” He argues that the material route is the most promising, because it works directly on the material conditions that physically facilitate, hinder, or constrain particular actions. At the same time, it is a contested route, because of its complex relations with human freedom and autonomy. With meticulous care, Ching Hung engages with the work of B.F. Skinner and Karl Popper to explore this tension.

<i>Design for Green</i> does not simply adopt Skinnerian techniques; instead, Ching Hung reinterprets them in light of contemporary ethical debates. The question is not just how to make people recycle more, consume less, or travel differently, but how to design reinforcement structures that respect self-determination, foster reflective choice, and cultivate durable ecological virtues rather than mere compliance. For this, Popper’s concept of “piecemeal engineering” offers a strong basis. No “Grand Design” will save us, but small and effective steps in the right direction that bring together human freedom and responsibility.

<i>Design for Green</i> is thus both a plea and a framework. It calls on designers, engineers, policymakers, and citizens to recognize the power of behavior-steering technologies and to take responsibility for their ecological and moral implications. It also offers important conceptual tools for distinguishing between coercive manipulation and responsible forms of behavioral influence. And, most importantly, Ching Hung has developed a “philosophy from technology” in addition to the philosophy of technology: an approach that takes actual technologies and their impacts as its starting point, recognizing that they do not merely “apply” or “embody” values, but play an active role in what these values actually mean and how they can play a role in society.

Ching Hung is an important new voice in the philosophy of technology and in Science and Technology Studies (STS). There is no doubt that we will hear more from him in the near future. I hope that his readers will take up the challenge he sets us: to design—and to demand—technologies that help us live well on a finite planet, guided not only by technical efficiency and human freedom but also by a sustained commitment to care for the more-than-human world.
